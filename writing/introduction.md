# Introduction

Large biobanks make it possible to study the determinants of disease at scale, but the most informative analyses often need data held by several institutions. Privacy law (e.g. GDPR), consent terms and data-access agreements usually prevent individual-level records from being shared between sites. Federated learning (FL) works around this by **(by sharing model parameters... or weights... etc)** leaving the data where it is. **[shorten here]** Each site (node) trains a model on its own data and shares only model parameters or gradients with a central server. The server combines these into a global model and sends it back to the nodes for the next round of training, and the cycle repeats until the global model converges.

Mendelian randomization (MR) runs into the same constraint but has largely solved it without FL. The standard MR estimators (inverse-variance weighted, MR-Egger, weighted median) need only the per-variant effect estimates and standard errors for the exposure and the outcome. These summary statistics are routinely shared. Two-sample MR can therefore take the exposure and outcome associations from different cohorts, and for linear models, fixed-effects meta-analysis of per-site estimates is asymptotically as efficient as pooling the individual-level data. Random-effects models and heterogeneity statistics also directly measure differences between sites, such as differences in ancestry, recruitment or phenotype definition.

This argument has limits. Summary statistics are sufficient only for linear, additive models. Some analyses need individual-level data and cannot be rebuilt from standard summary statistics: non-linear MR, which estimates effects within strata of the exposure; tests of gene–environment interaction; and consistent covariate adjustment across sites. For these analyses, FL is a legitimate alternative to pooling data. Our claim is therefore limited to linear MR based on summary statistics.

The same limit shows where FL is most useful: models whose parameters cannot be recovered from a small set of summary statistics. Non-linear prediction models, which can capture interactions and threshold effects across many phenotypes, are one such case. Here we develop a non-linear FL approach to identify phenotypic predictors of disease across biobanks that cannot share data. These predictors are associations and may reflect confounding or reverse causation. We therefore treat them as candidates for causal testing, not as causal findings, and the most promising can then be tested with summary-statistic MR. FL and MR thus play complementary roles: FL for discovery across separately held individual-level data, and MR for causal inference on what it finds.
