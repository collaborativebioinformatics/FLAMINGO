FedMR: exact federated Mendelian randomization

This branch adds a third federated route to FLAMINGO. Each biobank releases a few cross-product matrices, the coordinator sums them and solves, and the result is the pooled two-stage least squares fit to machine precision, with standard errors. Nothing is trained and no individual row moves.

The background in plain terms

Mendelian randomization

Mendelian randomization (MR) asks whether an exposure such as body-mass index causes an outcome such as heart disease. Ordinary regression cannot answer that, because unmeasured factors (diet, income, other conditions) push both the exposure and the outcome and the association gets confounded. MR uses genetic variants as a natural experiment: a person's alleles are fixed at conception and are not changed by later lifestyle, so variants that raise the exposure act like a random assignment to a slightly higher exposure. If people who carry those alleles also have more of the outcome, the exposure is likely to be causal. The variants are called instruments [1, 2, 3]. A recent clinical review of the method and its assumptions is Levin and Burgess [4].

Two-stage least squares

The standard estimator for MR with individual-level data is two-stage least squares (2SLS). Stage one predicts the exposure from the genetic variants: X ~ G. That prediction, written xhat, contains only the part of the exposure the genes explain, with the confounded part stripped out. Stage two regresses the outcome on that genetic prediction. The slope is the causal effect [5]. The strength of stage one is summarised by an F statistic; the rule of thumb is that F below 10 means "weak instruments" [6, 7], and weak instruments pull one-sample 2SLS back towards the confounded regression [8, 9].

Why federation is needed

Biobanks usually cannot share individual-level rows. Each site can run 2SLS on its own people and share the answer, but ten separate answers are less precise than one answer on all the people, and for anything beyond a straight line (a curved dose-response) each site's answer is too noisy to be useful. FLAMINGO simulates ten biobanks that share one true causal curve and compares ways of getting the pooled answer without moving rows:

  • Summary statistics. Each site publishes, per variant, its effect on the exposure and on the outcome. This is how MR is usually done in practice (the inverse-variance weighted estimator [10]). It recovers the average slope but carries no information about curvature [11].
  • Federated learning (FedAvg). A small neural network is trained across sites by exchanging model weights over many rounds. It can learn any curve shape, but it is an approximation and gives no standard error [12, 13]. The MR version uses two-stage residual inclusion as its second stage [14].
  • FedMR, new in this branch. Sites exchange the building blocks of the 2SLS formula once, and the coordinator gets the pooled 2SLS answer exactly.

Sufficient statistics

The 2SLS formula only ever uses the data through a few matrices of sums of products: things like the sum over people of (variant × variant), (variant × exposure) and (variant × outcome). A sum over all people is the sum over sites of each site's own sum. So each site computes its small matrices, sends them, and the coordinator adds them up and plugs them into the formula. No approximation is involved: the answer is the same, digit for digit, as if all the rows had been stacked in one file. Those matrices are the "sufficient statistics"; they are sufficient because the estimator needs nothing else. For twenty variants they are a 20-by-20 matrix and a few vectors per site. Pooling regressions from summed cross-products is an established idea in distributed biomedical analysis [15, 16] and in federated genome-wide association studies [17]; what is new here is applying it to one-sample MR with its first stage, robust errors and diagnostics.

Variables and their shapes

Subscript k marks a quantity that belongs to one site. Shapes use nk people at site k, m variants, q measured covariates, K sites and N = Σ nk people in total. In the checked-in sets m = 20, q = 0, K = 10, nk runs from 1,426 to 9,380 and N = 55,182. Every column of the site matrices is centred within its site before the products are formed, which is how the site intercept is absorbed.

Why this route

FLAMINGO already had two ways to run MR across sites without pooling rows. Per-SNP summary statistics recover the average slope but not the shape of a dose-response curve. The NVFlare FedAvg network recovers a flexible curve but gives no analytic confidence interval and is an approximation. FedMR fills the gap between them: a specified basis, an exact answer, and a confidence interval, in one or two communication rounds.

The idea in one line. With structural regressors W = [X, C] and instruments Z = [G, C], pooled 2SLS is θ = (B′A⁻¹B)⁻¹ B′A⁻¹c with A = Z′Z, B = Z′W, c = Z′Y. Every one of those is a sum over rows, so a sum over sites. Site k sends its own A_k, B_k, c_k (plus W′W, W′Y, Y′Y, n for the standard error); the coordinator adds and solves. Site intercepts are absorbed by centring each column within its site, so no dummy column is ever transmitted (the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem [18, 19]).

A second round gives the heteroskedasticity-robust covariance [20]: the coordinator broadcasts θ, each site returns H_k = Z′ diag(u²) Z, and the sandwich follows. First-stage F and partial R² need no extra round; with more than one endogenous regressor the per-column F is not the conditional F of Sanderson and Windmeijer [21], and the result says so.

Two protocols, not one aggregation

Whether sites hold the same variants matters. Real biobanks that have harmonized their genotypes can all contribute to the same variant-by-variant matrix. FLAMINGO's simulated sites each draw their own variants, so there is no shared matrix to sum; instead each site runs its own stage one and the federation only pools stage two. Both cases give the exact pooled answer, but they are different procedures and release different numbers.

The first review made the point that shared and site-specific instruments are different protocols. The package exposes them as such.

ProtocolWhenWhat leaves a siteRounds
SharedInstrumentFedMRthe same harmonized SNPs at every site, one effect allelecentred G′G, G′X, G′Y, X′X, X′Y, Y′Y, n1, +1 robust
LocalFirstStageFedMReach site's SNPs are its own variants (the repo's default simulator)the site fits X ~ [1, G] itself and releases nine scalars: the centred cross-products of xhat, X, Y, n, and the residual sums of squares of its first stage1, +1 robust

The local-first-stage protocol reproduces the repo's existing pooled fit (per-site first stages, site intercepts), which is the "concatenated" row on every forest plot. Both protocols also run a quadratic basis, W = [X, X², C] instrumented by [xhat, xhat², C], and a cross-fitted variant that solves Σ xhat_i^(−fold(i)) (Y_i − θ X_i) = 0, using the out-of-fold prediction as the instrument rather than as a substituted regressor. This is the split-sample idea of Angrist and Krueger [22] in the cross-fitting form used by double machine learning [23].

What is not claimed. This is distributed statistical estimation, not privacy-preserving estimation. The coordinator sees each site's released matrices. Secure aggregation could hide per-site values only for cells that several sites contribute to; a cell with one contributor is that site's number regardless of masking. No threat model, key exchange or collusion threshold is provided, and the plots say "federated: FedMR", not "private". Secure aggregation protocols that do provide these exist [24] and would be the starting point.

Verified

The claim to check is simple: the federated estimate must equal the pooled estimate, not approximately but to the limits of floating-point arithmetic (about 1e-16). The tests compare the full coefficient vector, both covariance matrices and the residual variance against a direct computation on stacked rows written independently of the package.

Seed sweep

A single simulated data set shows that FedMR equals pooling; it cannot show how the routes behave on average. The sweep repeats the whole simulation 100 times per setting with different random seeds, moving one design choice at a time away from the default (more or fewer sites, weaker instruments, more variants, unequal site sizes, shared variants with shifted allele frequencies, causal effects that differ between sites, pleiotropy) and records, for each route, how far off it is on average, how variable it is, and how often its confidence interval contains the truth.

100 seeds per level, ten sites of 500 to 5,000 people unless the axis says otherwise, a linear effect of 0.3. Bias and coverage are measured against the pooled-2SLS estimand, which under site-specific effects is the first-stage-weighted mean of the site effects, computed per replicate (the second review corrected an earlier n-weighted definition).

Bias with 95% coverage in parentheses. Full table in data/results/fedmr_sweep_summary.csv.

  • Sites and imbalance do not matter. With one linear effect, pooled 2SLS, site meta-analysis and per-SNP IVW are the same estimator up to weights, and FedMR is the pooled one exactly.
  • Weak instruments separate the routes, and only cross-fitting helps. At F near 4 every one-sample route leans about 0.025 toward the confounded value and coverage falls as low as 0.72. Cross-fitted FedMR removes the lean and restores coverage of 0.93 to 0.95, at the cost of a wider standard error. The hypothesis that out-of-fold instruments cut the one-sample lean holds in these settings.
  • Shared SNPs favour the pooled first stage. The global first stage has F about 150 where each site's own has about 15, so the site-level routes keep a lean of 0.010 the pooled and shared-instrument fits do not.
  • Effect heterogeneity is a question of estimand. Pooled, FedMR and site meta-analysis agree on the first-stage-weighted mean; per-SNP IVW drifts to −0.014.
  • Pleiotropy hurts every route equally. Exact federation does not protect against invalid instruments; that is a job for pleiotropy-robust estimators such as MR-Egger and the weighted median [25, 26].

Review history

Three reviews shaped the branch, and the plan document records them rather than erasing the earlier drafts.

  • Plan review. Shared and site-specific instruments became two explicit protocols instead of one column-alignment trick; site intercepts are absorbed by within-site centring rather than transmitted; generated instruments use a common column, because per-site columns change the quadratic estimator; the cross-fit estimating equation is written out; no explicit inverses, with rank and conditioning reported; the oracle-column check moved to the loader; privacy claims were dropped; the estimator became an importable package.
  • Second review. The sweep estimand became the pooled-2SLS limit rather than the n-weighted mean; the local protocol reports the F of the original SNP set (28.7 on the linear set) rather than the F of its single generated column (5,750); transport, serialization and simulator code were simplified; caches removed from git.
  • Code review. Cross-fit fold seeds through NVFlare now match the in-process protocol (they differed by 2e-3 before); shared sets are skipped for unsupported combinations; the NVFlare summary is keyed by cross-fit folds; the two generators now draw the shared SNP panel from one RNG stream, so the same seed gives the same panel.

Decisions to be aware of

  • Continuous outcomes only. Logistic and Cox IV models have no additive sufficient statistics; an iterative federated Newton protocol would be a later addition.
  • The FedAvg 2SRI route stays as its own family. It is the only route that fits a curve shape it was not given a basis for, such as the threshold plateau.
  • The NVFlare transport covers the shared protocol only with the linear basis and no cross-fitting; the in-process package covers everything.
  • On the shared-SNP set, the plot's concatenated row (per-site first stages) and the FedMR row (one shared first stage) are different estimators and differ by 0.002. The doc explains it.
